Hangsaman

by Shirley Jackson

Quotes

  • “Sometimes, with a vast aching heartbreak, the great, badly contained intentions of creation, the poignant searching longings of adolescence overwhelmed her, and shocked by her own capacity for creation, she held herself tight and unyielding, crying out silently something that might only be phrased as, ‘let me take, let me create.’” (23)
  • “The mountains, full-bosomed and rich, extended themselves to her in a surge of emotion, turning silently as she came, receiving her, and Natalie, her mouth against the grass and her eyes tearful from looking into the sun, took the mountains to herself and whispered, ‘Sister, sister.’ ‘Sister, sister,’ she said, and the mountains stirred, and answered.” (24)

Thoughts

  • Absolutely loved it, maybe best book so far this year?
    • Jackson's writing and imagery are gorgeous as ever
    • I loved the slow-paced, introspective, almost plotless vibes
    • Loved the blurring between Natalie's mind and reality, as if the two can never be fully extricated, because the only lens we have is Natalie's
  • This felt like the girl version of Catcher in the Rye
    • Teenage protagonist who's so convinced they're more real, more special, than everyone else – that nobody else could possibly understand – grappling with growing up and figuring out how to be a person who exists in this world
  • The characters are all so well realized without being overstated. Jackson is a master of subtle character writing
  • There's a dreamlike quality that suffuses the whole book and seeps into the way time and space are described as moving and passing with little conscious awareness on Natalie’s part
    • A metaphor for her trauma and depersonalization
    • She is never fully present in the world
  • I was also thinking a lot about space, liminal spaces, and the inside/outside binary: is inside safety or enclosure? Is outside freedom or danger?
    • Natalie finds safety in her room, in having a space of her own, but in doing so she cuts herself off from the outside world. In the dark, in the forest, she has the freedom to be with Tony, but there’s fear also
    • To be truly alone, outside of society’s gaze, is existentially horrifying. But to exist within society means constantly compromising your sense of self
  • In the end, Natalie rejects chaos (and lesbianism) and chooses to return to society, to begin learning how to exist as a person among people
    • But in the process, I think she loses something. She has emerged on the other side of the tunnel of adolescence – and homosexuality, I suppose, is treated as an adolescent fumbling, to be outgrown if one wants to be a productive member of society
    • Yet I don’t think Jackson’s message is so straightforward (by today’s standards it’s a bit homophobic, but this was the 1950s and it was incredibly difficult to exist as a lesbian adult)
    • I found the ending to be quite bittersweet: to grow up, Natalie must give up this part of herself because society is not yet ready to accept it
    • Existing as a person is a constant compromise between who you are and what society (and other people) expect of you. To me, the end of the book is Natalie learning how to make those compromises
