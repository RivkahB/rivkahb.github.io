Oranges Are Not the Only Fruit

by Jeanette Winterson

Quotes

  • “Of course that is not the whole story, but that is the way with stories; we make them what we will. It’s a way of explaining the universe while leaving the universe unexplained, it’s a way of keeping it all alive, not boxing it into time. Everyone who tells a story tells it differently, just to remind us that everyone sees it differently. Some people say there are true things to be found, some people say all kinds of things can be proved. I don’t believe them. The only thing for certain is how complicated it all is, like a string full of knots.” (94)
  • “And when I look at a history book and think of the imaginative effort it has taken to squeeze this oozing world between two boards and typeset, I am astonished. Perhaps the event has an unassailable truth. God saw it. God knows. But I am not God. And so when someone tells me what they heard or saw, I believe them, and I believe their friend who also saw, but not in the same way, and I can put these accounts together and I will not have a seamless wonder but a sandwich laced with mustard of my own.” (97)
  • “On the banks of the Euphrates find a secret garden cunningly walled. There is an entrance, but the entrance is guarded. There is no way in for you. Inside you will find every plant that grows growing circular-wise like a target. Close to the heart is a sundial and at the heart an orange tree. This fruit had tripped up athletes while others have healed their wounds. All true quests end in this garden, where the split fruit pours forth blood and the halved fruit is a full bowl for travelers and pilgrims. To eat of the fruit means to leave the garden because the fruit speaks of other things, other longings. So at dusk you say goodbye to the place you love, not knowing if you can ever return, knowing you can never return by the same way as this. It may be, some other day, that you will open a gate by chance, and find yourself again on the other side of the wall.” (125-126)
  • “Romantic love has been diluted into paperback form and has sold thousands and millions of copies. Somewhere it is still in the original, written on tablets of stone. I would cross seas and suffer sunstroke and give away all I have, but not for a man, because they want to be the destroyer and never the destroyed. That is why they are unfit for romantic love.” (175)
  • “The unknownness of my needs frightens me. I do not know how huge they are, or how high they are, I only know that they are not being met.” (175)

Thoughts

  • Definitely liked this better than The Stone Gods but not quite as much as The Passion
  • I love Winterson’s characterization here, she is able to make them feel fully realized in just a few sentences
    • Jeanette’s mother is a fanatic and a religious fundamentalist, yes, but she’s also a person – she has her foibles and quirks and often interprets scripture however she chooses
    • I’m guessing the strong character work comes from this book being heavily based on Winterson’s own childhood, but it really stands out in contrast to the flatness of the characters in The Stone Gods
  • What I really appreciate about this novel is the complexity of Jeanette’s relationship with her mother, with her community, with her church, with God
    • She doesn’t just turn around and leave, rejecting everything overnight. It takes her years, and she never completely severs the connection to her hometown and to her childhood. She always feels drawn back. It’s not that easy. Processing trauma, especially when it comes from the space that feels most safe, isn’t easy
    • She chooses to go back after being discovered and rejected because what else is she supposed to do? Where else is she supposed to go? She genuinely loves God and loves her church, and she finds ways to internally reconcile that with her love of women
  • It’s beautifully written and understated, getting across so much depth without having to articulate it explicitly. That perhaps even stating it outright would sacrifice some of the meaning, because Jeanette herself can barely make sense of what she’s going through
    • Every sentence, every word, feels both intentional and effortless, as Winterson’s prose almost always does
